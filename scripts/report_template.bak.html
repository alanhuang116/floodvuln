FloodVuln Global:

we actually built it.

A working implementation of the flood vulnerability platform described in the product white paper, calibrated against 1.86 million public flood-insurance claims and the published JRC global depth–damage database, with the benchmark programme run end to end. Five of the concept's claims did not survive contact with the evidence, including one on the website's front page. They are reported here rather than buried.

1. What was built

A Python package, floodvuln, implementing the five layers of the specification: an evidence register with licence classes, a harmonisation layer, a universal taxonomy with a national crosswalk, a hierarchical probabilistic core, and a prediction API that returns a damage distribution with provenance. It is calibrated on real data and validated by a pre-specified benchmark programme, not by assertion.

  • Against a single depth–damage curve fitted on the same training data, which is conventional practice at its best, it [[imp_ev_det]].
  • In a withheld region with no local evidence at all, the archetype prior still [[imp_rg]] against the published global curve. That is the day-one number for a new market.

Read this before the results

Five claims in the product concept are not supported by the data, and two of them are load bearing: that local calibration narrows uncertainty, and that it improves prediction unconditionally. Both are wrong as stated. They are set out in section 8 and the website has been corrected. A platform whose selling point is transparent benchmarking does not get to quietly drop the benchmarks that went against it.

2. Scope, and what “all available datasets” actually meant

The brief asked for calibration against all available datasets. What is genuinely available to an automated build, without a licence negotiation or a signed data agreement, turned out to be a much smaller set than the white paper's source inventory implies. That gap is itself a finding, because it is the same gap a real product team hits in month one.

On not scraping

Two of the missing sources are public documents behind a bot filter, and two require a licence. A build script that routed around either would have produced a better-looking report and an unusable product. The register above records the refusal rather than hiding it.

3. Literature, and how it changed the design

The review was run to settle specific design questions, not to produce a citation list.

The distributional form

Wing et al. (2020) analysed over two million NFIP claims and found that observed losses are not a monotonic function of depth in the individual, and are better described by a beta distribution that is frequently bimodal at a given depth. That is a direct argument for a bounded distribution with mass at the endpoints. Recent work has converged on exactly this: the 2026 NHESS study of flood damage functions for rice uses a Bayesian zero-and-one-inflated beta and scores it with CRPS, MAE and mean bias error — the same family and the same metrics adopted here. That study also reports the ordering deterministic stage–damage < probabilistic stage–damage < Bayesian regression < random forest, which sets a realistic expectation for how much a probabilistic model should win by.

The pooling structure

Wagenaar et al. (2018) showed that multivariable damage models transfer better when updated with data from the destination region, which is the case for partial pooling rather than either a fixed global curve or an unpooled local refit. Gerl et al. (2016), reviewing 61 sources and nearly a thousand functions, found strong heterogeneity and limited explicit validation, which is the case for a harmonisation layer with provenance.

The multivariable claim

Wagenaar et al. (2017) reported roughly a 20% mean-absolute-error reduction from adding hazard and building variables to a depth-only model. That claim was treated as a hypothesis to test rather than a result to repeat, and section 8 reports what happened.

4. Data acquisition

The NFIP claims file was pulled through the OpenFEMA API using keyset pagination on the record id, which is resumable and cannot silently truncate at a $skip ceiling. Two pulls were made: the primary filter requires a reported water depth, a positive assessed building damage amount and at least one usable value denominator; a second pull recovers records with zero assessed building damage, because the primary filter would otherwise remove the entire zero point mass and make the zero-inflation component unidentifiable by construction.

The second pull returned only 4,476 records against 1.92 million in the first. That number is a result, not a footnote, and section 8 returns to it.

The JRC database was downloaded as the published workbook and parsed into a tidy curve table. Its Europe residential curve reproduces the published values exactly (0, 0.25, 0.40, 0.50, 0.60, 0.75, 0.85, 0.95, 1.00 at 0 to 6 m), which is the curve-level reproduction check the specification asks for.

5. Harmonisation: three problems that decide the answer

The specification calls depth-reference harmonisation critical. It understates the case. Three ambiguities in the source data each move the fitted curve more than any modelling choice made later.

5.1 The units are not what the dictionary says

OpenFEMA documents waterDepth as inches, noting that some measurements were provided in feet. The raw distribution says otherwise: 85.5% of positive values fall in 1–10, with secondary spikes at 12, 24 and 36. Three independent tests were run.

  • Ratio test. On the 66,117 claims carrying both waterDepth and interiorWaterDepth (unambiguously inches), the ratio is 10–11 for reported values of 1–11, and exactly 1.0 across the bands 12, 13–24 and 25–48. 73% of the paired mass is consistent with feet.
  • Digit preference. 85.5% of positive values lie in 1–10, the signature of whole-feet entry; only 4.7% are exact multiples of 12.
  • Damage response. On the cleanest subset — single-family, one storey, n = 372,253 — the feet reading gives a mean damage ratio rising monotonically from 0.196 at 0 ft to 0.718 at 10 ft, with a linear correlation to depth of 0.369 against 0.216 under the inches reading.

Adopted rule: values with magnitude at or below 11 are feet; 12 and above are inches; exactly 12 is flagged ambiguous and excluded from primary calibration, because it is the one point where the two populations overlap and its mean damage ratio (0.49) sits between the 1 ft value (0.25) and the 10 ft value (0.72), which is the signature of a mixture.

An undocumented sentinel, found by its physics

The value 99 appears on 8,817 claims with a mean damage ratio of 0.205, against 0.539, 0.602 and 0.712 at its immediate neighbours 96, 108 and 120 (that is, 8, 9 and 10 ft). A 99 inch flood cannot be less damaging than a 96 inch one, so 99 is a not-reported code that the data dictionary does not mention. Before it was caught, it dragged the fitted curve down across the 2.2–3.1 m band, where the observed mean read 0.366; with the sentinel removed the same band reads 0.476.

5.2 The datum differs between fields

waterDepth follows adjuster practice relative to the lowest floor. interiorWaterDepth is relative to the lowest adjacent grade. Mixing them shifts the whole curve by the floor-above-grade height. Where the elevation fields permit, the interior reading is preferred and the datum corrected; this applies to 3.3% of records and is flagged.

5.3 The damage basis is mixed

buildingDamageAmount is an actual cash value figure. buildingReplacementCost is a replacement cost. Dividing one by the other mixes bases and biases the damage ratio low. The ACV-consistent denominator buildingPropertyValue is used where present (99.5% of ingested records, and 99.9% of those for which a damage ratio could be computed at all) and the replacement-cost denominator is converted by an empirically estimated factor and flagged where it is not.

5.4 An undocumented category code

foundationType is populated on all 2.7 million records, but 1.54 million of them carry the value 0, which is not in the published dictionary (it lists 1–6). It is a not-reported code. It is mapped to UNK and flagged as assumed rather than silently folded into SLAB, which would have inflated the slab-on-grade sample roughly eightfold — 219,591 genuine slab records against 1,544,359 not-reported ones — almost entirely out of missing data.

6. The model

Damage ratio D given depth and archetype follows a zero-and-one-inflated beta:

P(D = 0) = p₀  ·  P(D = 1) = (1 − p₀) p₁  ·  D | 0 < D < 1 ~ Beta(μφ, (1−μ)φ)

Each linear predictor is an intercept plus a non-negatively weighted sum of logistic ramps in depth. Monotonicity therefore holds by construction for any parameter values, satisfying the specification's physical-plausibility requirement without a post-hoc check: expected damage cannot fall as inundation deepens, P(no damage) cannot rise, and P(total loss) cannot fall.

Exact aggregation

The ZOIB log-likelihood separates into a categorical part over {0, (0,1), 1} and a beta part over the interior, and the beta density depends on the interior observations only through n, ∑log x and ∑log(1−x). Binning by depth therefore yields sufficient statistics rather than an approximation. Fitting 1.86 million claims costs the same as fitting a hundred cells, which is what makes the twenty-fold benchmark programme affordable. A property test confirms the binned likelihood is stable to within 5×10⁻³ relative across a 25-fold change in bin width.

Hierarchical partial pooling

Three levels: global, archetype, and archetype × region. Each level's offset is fitted under a Gaussian prior whose scale is estimated across units by moment matching. The shrinkage is not imposed by a formula; it falls out of maximising the penalised local likelihood. The behaviour can be read directly off the fitted model: for single-family slab dwellings, Florida (n = 63,780), Texas (41,948) and Louisiana (31,927) carry substantial offsets, while Alaska (7), Utah (4) and the Northern Marianas (1) have offsets collapsed to within 0.02 of the archetype value.

A variance component that had to be capped

The moment estimator returned a between-unit scale of 3.4 and 7.5 for the two inflation components. That is not evidence of real between-group variation; it is an unidentified parameter wandering, because the zero and one components sit near the boundary in claims data. Left uncapped it would have switched shrinkage off exactly where shrinkage is most needed. The ceiling is documented in the code with this reasoning attached.

Uncertainty, separated

Aleatory spread is the fitted ZOIB itself. Epistemic spread is the posterior of the group offset under the shrinkage prior, Cov = (H + diag(1/τ²))⁻¹, so a region with one observation inherits the prior variance and a region with sixty thousand collapses to the likelihood. This matters more than it sounds: with 1.86 million records the global parameter vector is pinned down and contributes under 0.001, so an implementation that reported only parameter uncertainty would have reported approximately zero everywhere and called it calibrated.

Parameter recovery

Before touching real data the fitter was tested against synthetic data drawn from a known ZOIB with 200,000 observations. Maximum absolute error on the recovered mean curve across −0.5 to 5 m was 0.0019, with p₀ and p₁ recovered to three decimal places. Twenty-two property tests cover monotonicity under random parameters, quantile–CDF inversion, closed-form moments against simulation, CRPS reduction to absolute error for a degenerate forecast, and every harmonisation rule.

7. Results

7.1 The archetypes separate, and in the right order

Fitted mean damage ratio at 0.91 m above the lowest floor: manufactured home 0.517, single-family one-storey slab 0.451, one-storey crawlspace 0.363, two-storey slab 0.325, elevated with enclosure 0.261, two-storey with basement 0.254. The ordering is not imposed. It reproduces the physical expectation that a second storey puts more value above the water and that elevation works, and it recovers the modifiers Wing et al. identified as material: occupancy, storeys and foundation.

7.2 The published global prior, measured

The JRC North America residential curve is the fallback the specification nominates for countries without local functions. Against 1.86 million US claims it runs high across the usable depth range, by roughly 0.2 to 0.3 in absolute damage ratio between 0.5 and 3 m. This is not a criticism of a global product used as intended, and the JRC curves are normalised to a different maximum-damage convention. It is the size of the correction that a local calibration layer exists to supply, and it is measurable.

8. Seven findings that change the product

1

Local calibration relocates the distribution. It does not sharpen it.

This contradicts the concept's central visual, the calibration ladder captioned “more evidence narrows uncertainty”. At 0.61 m for a single-family slab dwelling, the P10–P90 width is 0.670 with no archetype information, 0.723 with the archetype, and 0.731 with 63,780 local Florida claims. It gets marginally wider, because the width is dominated by genuine building-to-building variability that no amount of local data removes. What local evidence does buy is the epistemic component, and that falls from 0.111 at the published prior to 0.001 with a large local sample — a factor of about a hundred. The honest claim is: local calibration buys accuracy and calibration, not sharpness. The website has been corrected and now reports the two components separately.

2

The zero point mass cannot be estimated from claims data.

A building that is inundated and suffers no damage rarely becomes an insurance claim, so it never enters the file. The second pull recovered only 4,476 zero-damage records against 1.92 million positive ones, and the fitted P(D = 0) is therefore 0.1–2% — conditional on a claim being filed, and a lower bound on the quantity the product advertises. Estimating P(no damage | inundated) requires an exposure file listing every building in the flood footprint, which is a client input, not a public one. This strengthens rather than weakens the commercial case for the G5 client-view rung, but the API now returns the conditioning as an explicit warning instead of a clean number.

3

What is missing is the event, not the building.

Per-event bias is large and systematic: Katrina −0.271 at archetype level, Harvey −0.087, generic flooding +0.068. Region calibration fixes part of Katrina (−0.271 to −0.186, because Louisiana genuinely differs) but nothing of Harvey, because Texas averages across many events. Two experiments separate the explanations. Adding the only additional hazard variable NFIP carries at scale changed MAE by 0.0000. Granting the model a single four-parameter event-level shift — an oracle, not a forecast — cut CRPS by 22.8%, removed the bias entirely and repaired coverage from 0.726 to 0.826 against a nominal 0.80.

[[cov_table]]

The roadmap consequence is concrete: an event-level random effect, updated from the first days of claims, is worth more than any additional per-building covariate available here. That is also a saleable capability, because it is exactly what post-event reserving needs.

4

Region calibration does not generalise to a new event, and can hurt.

This one was not expected. Averaged over eleven withheld events the archetype model scores CRPS [[crps_g1]] and the region-calibrated model [[crps_g4]] — the extra local layer [[g4_vs_g1]], and coverage falls from [[cov_g1]] to [[cov_g4]] against a nominal 0.80. Local calibration made the forecast worse.

The mechanism follows from finding 3. A region offset fitted on that region's history is partly absorbing the event composition of that history: Louisiana's offset is substantially Katrina, Texas's is substantially Harvey and Allison. Applied to a genuinely new event it transfers the wrong severity. Within-region random hold-out, where the future resembles the past, still shows the expected gain — that is what the learning curve in section 7.6 measures — so the honest statement is conditional:

The corrected claim

Local calibration improves prediction for the kind of events that region has already seen. It does not, on this evidence, improve prediction for a new event of different character, and it can degrade coverage. Selling it as an unconditional improvement would not survive a portfolio blind test on a novel event.

The fix is not to abandon local calibration but to separate the two things it is currently conflating: a persistent regional building-stock effect, which should transfer, and an event-severity effect, which should not. That is the same event-level random effect finding 3 already prices at roughly 23% CRPS.

5

Partial pooling under-performed an unpooled refit — diagnosed, and fixed.

The learning curve inverted the expected ordering. From about ten local records upward, a full unpooled refit on the same small sample beat the hierarchical model, which is the opposite of what partial pooling exists to do. That is a defect in the implementation, not in the concept, and it was worth chasing rather than reporting as a curiosity.

The diagnosis is specific. Louisiana's observed response for the largest archetype runs 0.192, 0.449, 0.668, 0.812 at 0, 0.61, 1.22 and 2.44 m — materially steeper than the national average. The four-parameter offset can move the level of a curve but not its slope, so it compromises: it over-predicted at 0 m (0.289 against 0.192 observed) and under-predicted at depth (0.714 against 0.812). An unpooled refit, free to change the shape, tracked the data almost exactly. Regional variation lives in the slope of the depth response, and the hierarchy only parameterised the level.

The fix is a fifth offset component that scales the depth response by a strictly positive multiplier, so monotonicity is preserved by construction exactly as before. Measured on a clean split with the archetype level refitted without Louisiana:

The shape offset improves CRPS by about 4.2% at every sample size, and at 10 and 25 local records it now beats the unpooled refit — restoring the ordering the product claims, precisely in the sparse-data regime where the claim matters. By a few hundred records the two converge, which is the correct behaviour: with enough local data there is nothing left for a prior to contribute. The shipped model retains the level-only offset; the shape component is implemented and validated but not yet refitted across all 4,483 units, which is the first item on the roadmap.

6

The multivariable-hazard claim does not replicate on this dataset.

floodWaterDuration is recorded on 1.6% of claims, and within every depth band the recorded-duration subset shows lower mean damage than the unrecorded majority (0.294 against 0.426 above 1.2 m). That is a non-random recording process, not a physical signal. The cited ~20% MAE improvement from multivariable models is not contradicted — it was obtained on survey data with genuine duration and velocity measurements — but it cannot be claimed on the strength of NFIP. The product should say “multivariable where the data support it” and name the datasets that do.

7

Enforced monotonicity costs accuracy at depth.

Observed mean damage for single-family slab dwellings plateaus above about 1.7 m and the binned means then wander (0.504 at 1.66 m, 0.447 at 2.39 m, 0.505 at 3.15 m) on samples of a few thousand. The monotone constraint holds the fitted curve rising, so it over-predicts by up to 0.15 in that range. The specification allows evidence-driven exceptions to monotonicity; whether this qualifies is a judgement about whether the plateau is real physics (a one-storey house is destroyed once, and deeper water adds nothing) or thin-sample noise. It is most likely real, and the fix is a saturating ceiling per archetype rather than abandoning monotonicity.

9. Limitations

  • One country. Calibration is US-only. The taxonomy and crosswalk are built to take other national schemes, and none have been ingested.
  • Insured stock only. NFIP policyholders are not a random sample of exposed buildings. Selection runs through mandatory purchase requirements, flood-zone designation and price.
  • Adjuster-assessed damage. The target is an adjuster's actual-cash-value estimate, not an engineering survey. It carries adjuster practice, and practice varies by event and carrier.
  • Depth is inferred for 96.7% of records. The unit rule is well evidenced but it is a rule, and 1.3% of records were dropped as ambiguous.
  • Construction material is assumed. NFIP does not record it. Timber frame is assigned to single-family residential and flagged; the L3 level of the taxonomy is otherwise empty.
  • Empirical Bayes, not full posterior. Variance components are point-estimated and the offset posterior is a Laplace approximation. A full MCMC treatment would widen the epistemic intervals somewhat, most at small n.
  • No contents, no business interruption. The fitted model covers building damage only.

10. What to build next, in order

  1. Refit the shape offset across all 4,483 hierarchy units. It is implemented and validated at about 4.2% CRPS with the sparse-data ordering restored (section 8.5); it needs a full refit and a re-run of the benchmark programme before it ships.
  2. Event-level random effects with an in-event update path. Section 8.3 puts an upper bound of about 23% CRPS on this, the largest single gain identified, and it is also the fix for the region-generalisation failure in 8.4.
  3. Per-archetype saturation ceilings to resolve the monotonicity conflict at depth.
  4. An exposure-file ingestion path, which is the only way to make P(no damage) a real number rather than a conditional one.
  5. A second country. Japan's MLIT damage rates and the Australian state guidance are published tables and would test the crosswalk properly.
  6. Licence negotiations for HOWAS21 and the Multi-Coloured Manual, which are the two sources that would most improve the non-US prior.
  7. Full Bayesian inference on the hierarchy once the structure stops changing.

python scripts/fetch_nfip.py

python scripts/depth_units.py

python experiments/run_calibration.py

python scripts/make_figures.py
python scripts/export_to_web.py && python scripts/update_site.py
python scripts/make_report.py
python tests/test_core.py && node tests/test_js_parity.js <site.html>

Every prediction carries a model version and an evidence snapshot hash over the source files, so a result can be tied to the exact inputs that produced it. The JavaScript engine shipped in the website is checked against the Python engine on 48 cases at each release; the current worst divergence is 1.2×10⁻⁷.
