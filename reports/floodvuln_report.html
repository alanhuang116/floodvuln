The flood vulnerability platform from the product white paper, implemented and calibrated against 1.86 million public flood-insurance claims and the published JRC global depth–damage database, with the full benchmark programme run out of sample. It beats the standard global fallback by 40–54% on CRPS. Section 8 sets out what the validation established, and where the next gains are.

1. What was built

A Python package, floodvuln, implementing the five layers of the specification: an evidence register with licence classes, a harmonisation layer, a universal taxonomy with a national crosswalk, a hierarchical probabilistic core, and a prediction API that returns a damage distribution with provenance. It is calibrated on real data and validated by a pre-specified benchmark programme, not by assertion.

Hierarchy units fitted4,625142 archetype groups and 4,483 archetype × region units.
Benchmark folds2011 withheld events and 9 withheld states, plus a learning curve.

The headline quantitative results, all out of sample:

  • Against the published JRC global prior — the standard fallback where no local function exists — the engine reduces CRPS by 40.2% on withheld events. That baseline also carries a calibration slope of 0.360, where 1.0 is correct, and a bias of +0.0910; both are corrected.
  • Against a single depth–damage curve fitted on the same training data, which is conventional practice at its best, it reduces CRPS by 35.4%.
  • In a withheld region with no local evidence at all, the archetype prior still reduces CRPS by 54.1% against the published global curve. That is the day-one number for a new market.

On not scraping

Two missing sources sit behind a bot filter, two require a licence. Routing around either would have produced a better-looking report and an unusable product.

3. Literature, and how it changed the design

The review settled three design questions.

The distributional form

Wing et al. (2020), on over two million NFIP claims, found observed losses are not monotonic in depth at the individual level and are better described by a beta distribution, frequently bimodal at a given depth — an argument for a bounded distribution with mass at the endpoints. The 2026 NHESS study of rice damage functions uses the same zero-and-one-inflated beta family and the same CRPS/MAE/bias metrics adopted here, and reports the ordering deterministic < probabilistic < Bayesian < random forest, which sets a realistic expectation for the size of the win.

The pooling structure

Wagenaar et al. (2018): multivariable models transfer better when updated with destination-region data — the case for partial pooling over either a fixed global curve or an unpooled local refit. Gerl et al. (2016): 61 sources, nearly a thousand functions, strong heterogeneity, limited validation — the case for a harmonisation layer with provenance.

The multivariable claim

Wagenaar et al. (2017) reported roughly 20% MAE reduction from adding hazard and building variables. Treated here as a hypothesis to test, not a result to repeat; see section 8.6.

4. Data acquisition

The NFIP claims file was pulled through the OpenFEMA API using keyset pagination on the record id, which is resumable and cannot silently truncate at a $skip ceiling. Two pulls were made: the primary filter requires a reported water depth, a positive assessed building damage amount and at least one usable value denominator; a second pull recovers records with zero assessed building damage, because the primary filter would otherwise remove the entire zero point mass and make the zero-inflation component unidentifiable by construction.

The second pull returned only 4,476 records against 1.92 million in the first. That number is a result, not a footnote, and section 8 returns to it.

The JRC database was downloaded as the published workbook and parsed into a tidy curve table. Its Europe residential curve reproduces the published values exactly (0, 0.25, 0.40, 0.50, 0.60, 0.75, 0.85, 0.95, 1.00 at 0 to 6 m), which is the curve-level reproduction check the specification asks for.

5. Harmonisation: three problems that decide the answer

Three ambiguities in the source data each move the fitted curve more than any modelling choice made later.

5.1 The units are not what the dictionary says

OpenFEMA documents waterDepth as inches, noting that some measurements were provided in feet. The raw distribution says otherwise: 85.5% of positive values fall in 1–10, with secondary spikes at 12, 24 and 36. Three independent tests were run.

  • Ratio test. On the 66,117 claims carrying both waterDepth and interiorWaterDepth (unambiguously inches), the ratio is 10–11 for reported values of 1–11, and exactly 1.0 across the bands 12, 13–24 and 25–48. 73% of the paired mass is consistent with feet.
  • Digit preference. 85.5% of positive values lie in 1–10, the signature of whole-feet entry; only 4.7% are exact multiples of 12.
  • Damage response. On the cleanest subset — single-family, one storey, n = 372,253 — the feet reading gives a mean damage ratio rising monotonically from 0.196 at 0 ft to 0.718 at 10 ft, with a linear correlation to depth of 0.369 against 0.216 under the inches reading.

Adopted rule: magnitude at or below 11 is feet; 12 and above is inches; exactly 12 is flagged ambiguous and excluded, being the one point where the populations overlap — its mean damage ratio (0.49) sits between the 1 ft value (0.25) and the 10 ft value (0.72).

An undocumented sentinel, found by its physics

The value 99 appears on 8,817 claims with a mean damage ratio of 0.205, against 0.539, 0.602 and 0.712 at its immediate neighbours 96, 108 and 120 (that is, 8, 9 and 10 ft). A 99 inch flood cannot be less damaging than a 96 inch one, so 99 is a not-reported code that the data dictionary does not mention. Before it was caught, it dragged the fitted curve down across the 2.2–3.1 m band, where the observed mean read 0.366; with the sentinel removed the same band reads 0.476.

5.2 The datum differs between fields

waterDepth follows adjuster practice relative to the lowest floor. interiorWaterDepth is relative to the lowest adjacent grade. Mixing them shifts the whole curve by the floor-above-grade height. Where the elevation fields permit, the interior reading is preferred and the datum corrected; this applies to 3.3% of records and is flagged.

5.3 The damage basis is mixed

buildingDamageAmount is an actual cash value figure. buildingReplacementCost is a replacement cost. Dividing one by the other mixes bases and biases the damage ratio low. The ACV-consistent denominator buildingPropertyValue is used where present (99.5% of ingested records, and 99.9% of those for which a damage ratio could be computed at all) and the replacement-cost denominator is converted by an empirically estimated factor and flagged where it is not.

5.4 An undocumented category code

foundationType is populated on all 2.7 million records, but 1.54 million of them carry the value 0, which is not in the published dictionary (it lists 1–6). It is a not-reported code. It is mapped to UNK and flagged as assumed rather than silently folded into SLAB, which would have inflated the slab-on-grade sample roughly eightfold — 219,591 genuine slab records against 1,544,359 not-reported ones — almost entirely out of missing data.

6. The model

Damage ratio D given depth and archetype follows a zero-and-one-inflated beta:

P(D = 0) = p₀  ·  P(D = 1) = (1 − p₀) p₁  ·  D | 0 < D < 1 ~ Beta(μφ, (1−μ)φ)

Each linear predictor is an intercept plus a non-negatively weighted sum of logistic ramps in depth. Monotonicity therefore holds by construction for any parameter values, satisfying the specification's physical-plausibility requirement without a post-hoc check: expected damage cannot fall as inundation deepens, P(no damage) cannot rise, and P(total loss) cannot fall.

Exact aggregation

The ZOIB log-likelihood separates into a categorical part over {0, (0,1), 1} and a beta part whose density depends on the interior observations only through n, ∑log x and ∑log(1−x). Binning by depth therefore yields sufficient statistics, not an approximation: fitting 1.86 million claims costs what fitting a hundred cells costs, which is what makes a twenty-fold benchmark programme affordable. A property test confirms stability to 5×10⁻³ across a 25-fold change in bin width.

Hierarchical partial pooling

Three levels: global, archetype, and archetype × region. Each level's offset is fitted under a Gaussian prior whose scale is estimated across units by moment matching. The shrinkage is not imposed by a formula; it falls out of maximising the penalised local likelihood. The behaviour can be read directly off the fitted model: for single-family slab dwellings, Florida (n = 63,780), Texas (41,948) and Louisiana (31,927) carry substantial offsets, while Alaska (7), Utah (4) and the Northern Marianas (1) have offsets collapsed to within 0.02 of the archetype value.

A variance component that had to be capped

The moment estimator returned a between-unit scale of 3.4 and 7.5 for the two inflation components — not real between-group variation but an unidentified parameter wandering, because those components sit near the boundary in claims data. Uncapped it would switch shrinkage off exactly where shrinkage is needed.

Uncertainty, separated

Aleatory spread is the fitted ZOIB. Epistemic spread is the posterior of the group offset under the shrinkage prior, Cov = (H + diag(1/τ²))⁻¹, so a region with one observation inherits the prior variance and one with sixty thousand collapses to the likelihood. With 1.86 million records the global parameter vector contributes under 0.001 — an implementation reporting only parameter uncertainty would report approximately zero everywhere and call it calibrated.

Parameter recovery

Tested first against synthetic data from a known ZOIB, 200,000 observations: maximum absolute error on the recovered mean curve across −0.5 to 5 m was 0.0019, with p₀ and p₁ recovered to three decimals. Twenty-two property tests cover monotonicity under random parameters, quantile–CDF inversion, closed-form moments against simulation, CRPS reduction to absolute error, and every harmonisation rule.

7. Results

7.1 The archetypes separate, and in the right order

Fitted mean damage ratio at 0.91 m above the lowest floor: manufactured home 0.517, one-storey slab 0.451, one-storey crawlspace 0.363, two-storey slab 0.325, elevated with enclosure 0.261, two-storey with basement 0.254. The ordering is not imposed. It reproduces the expectation that a second storey puts more value above the water and that elevation works, and recovers the modifiers Wing et al. found material: occupancy, storeys, foundation.

7.2 The published global prior, measured

The JRC North America residential curve is the specification's nominated fallback for countries without local functions. Against 1.86 million US claims it runs high by roughly 0.2 to 0.3 in absolute damage ratio between 0.5 and 3 m. That is not a criticism of a global product used as intended — the JRC curves use a different maximum-damage convention — it is the size of the correction a calibration layer exists to supply, and it is measurable.

7.3 The distribution at fixed depth

The observed spread at a given depth is very wide, which is the empirical claim the whole product rests on. The fitted beta component tracks the interior shape.

7.4 Leave-one-event-out

Eleven named events withheld one at a time, the model refitted without each. Deterministic curves are scored as degenerate forecasts, so their CRPS is their mean absolute error.

7.7 Calibration diagnostics

The probability integral transform should be uniform under a calibrated forecast. On a withheld event it is not, and the direction is informative: observed damage falls in the upper part of the predicted distribution, so the model under-predicts. Region calibration barely improves the tilt — the clue behind section 8.3.

8. What the validation established

1

Local calibration relocates the distribution rather than sharpening it.

This contradicts the concept's central visual, the calibration ladder captioned “more evidence narrows uncertainty”. At 0.61 m for a single-family slab dwelling the P10–P90 width is 0.670 with no archetype information, 0.723 with the archetype and 0.731 with 63,780 local Florida claims — marginally wider, because the width is building-to-building variability that local data cannot remove. What local evidence buys is the epistemic component, which falls from 0.111 to 0.001, a factor of about a hundred. Local calibration buys accuracy and calibration, not sharpness. The site now reports the two components separately.

2

The zero point mass requires exposure data, not claims data.

A building inundated without damage rarely becomes a claim, so it never enters the file. The second pull recovered 4,476 zero-damage records against 1.92 million positive ones, so the fitted P(D = 0) of 0.1–2% is conditional on a claim being filed — a lower bound on the quantity the product advertises. Estimating P(no damage | inundated) needs an exposure file listing every building in the footprint, which is a client input. That strengthens the commercial case for the G5 client-view rung; the API now returns the conditioning as an explicit warning rather than a clean number.

3

The largest remaining gain is an event-level effect, worth about 23% CRPS.

Per-event bias is large and systematic: Katrina −0.271 at archetype level, Harvey −0.087, generic flooding +0.068. Region calibration fixes part of Katrina (−0.271 to −0.186, because Louisiana genuinely differs) but nothing of Harvey, because Texas averages across many events. Two experiments separate the explanations. Adding the only additional hazard variable NFIP carries at scale changed MAE by 0.0000. Granting the model a single four-parameter event-level shift — an oracle, not a forecast — cut CRPS by 22.8%, removed the bias entirely and repaired coverage from 0.726 to 0.826 against a nominal 0.80.

4

Region calibration transfers within a region, not across event types.

This one was not expected. Averaged over eleven withheld events the archetype model scores CRPS 0.1489 and the region-calibrated model 0.1540 — the extra local layer increases CRPS by 3.4% (worse), and coverage falls from 0.786 to 0.753 against a nominal 0.80. Local calibration made the forecast worse.

The mechanism follows from finding 3: a region offset partly absorbs the event composition of that region's history — Louisiana's offset is substantially Katrina, Texas's substantially Harvey and Allison — so it transfers the wrong severity to a new event. Within-region random hold-out, where the future resembles the past, still shows the expected gain (section 7.6). The claim is therefore conditional:

Scope of the claim

Local calibration improves prediction for the kind of events a region has already seen. On this evidence it does not improve prediction for a new event of different character, and can degrade coverage. The claim is stated conditionally on the site and in the pilot protocol.

The fix is to separate what the offset currently conflates: a persistent regional building-stock effect, which should transfer, and an event-severity effect, which should not. That is the event-level random effect finding 3 prices at roughly 23% CRPS.

5

A shape offset in the hierarchy, worth 4.2% CRPS.

The learning curve inverted the expected ordering: from about ten local records upward a full unpooled refit beat the hierarchical model, the opposite of what partial pooling exists to do. A defect in the implementation, not the concept.

Louisiana's observed response for the largest archetype runs 0.192, 0.449, 0.668, 0.812 at 0, 0.61, 1.22 and 2.44 m — materially steeper than national. A four-parameter offset moves a curve's level but not its slope, so it compromised: over-predicting at 0 m (0.289 against 0.192) and under-predicting at depth (0.714 against 0.812). An unpooled refit, free to change shape, tracked the data almost exactly. Regional variation lives in the slope, and the hierarchy only parameterised the level.

The fix is a fifth offset component that scales the depth response by a strictly positive multiplier, so monotonicity is preserved by construction exactly as before. Measured on a clean split with the archetype level refitted without Louisiana:

6

Multivariable hazard needs data NFIP does not carry.

floodWaterDuration is recorded on 1.6% of claims, and within every depth band the recorded subset shows lower mean damage than the unrecorded majority (0.294 against 0.426 above 1.2 m) — a non-random recording process, not a physical signal. The cited ~20% MAE improvement is not contradicted; it came from survey data with genuine duration and velocity. It simply cannot be claimed on NFIP. The product should say “multivariable where the data support it” and name those datasets.

7

Monotonicity trades accuracy at depth, addressable per archetype.

Observed mean damage for single-family slab dwellings plateaus above about 1.7 m and then wanders (0.504 at 1.66 m, 0.447 at 2.39 m, 0.505 at 3.15 m) on samples of a few thousand. The monotone constraint holds the fitted curve rising, so it over-predicts by up to 0.15 there. The plateau is most likely real — a one-storey house is destroyed once and deeper water adds nothing — and the fix is a per-archetype saturating ceiling, not abandoning monotonicity.

9. Limitations

  • One country. Calibration is US-only. The taxonomy and crosswalk are built to take other national schemes, and none have been ingested.
  • Insured stock only. NFIP policyholders are not a random sample of exposed buildings. Selection runs through mandatory purchase requirements, flood-zone designation and price.
  • Adjuster-assessed damage. The target is an adjuster's actual-cash-value estimate, not an engineering survey. It carries adjuster practice, and practice varies by event and carrier.
  • Depth is inferred for 96.7% of records. The unit rule is well evidenced but it is a rule, and 1.3% of records were dropped as ambiguous.
  • Construction material is assumed. NFIP does not record it. Timber frame is assigned to single-family residential and flagged; the L3 level of the taxonomy is otherwise empty.
  • Empirical Bayes, not full posterior. Variance components are point-estimated and the offset posterior is a Laplace approximation. A full MCMC treatment would widen the epistemic intervals somewhat, most at small n.
  • No contents, no business interruption. The fitted model covers building damage only.

10. What to build next, in order

  1. Refit the shape offset across all 4,483 hierarchy units. It is implemented and validated at about 4.2% CRPS with the sparse-data ordering restored (section 8.5); it needs a full refit and a re-run of the benchmark programme before it ships.
  2. Event-level random effects with an in-event update path. Section 8.3 puts an upper bound of about 23% CRPS on this, the largest single gain identified, and it is also the fix for the region-generalisation failure in 8.4.
  3. Per-archetype saturation ceilings to resolve the monotonicity conflict at depth.
  4. An exposure-file ingestion path, which is the only way to make P(no damage) a real number rather than a conditional one.
  5. A second country. Japan's MLIT damage rates and the Australian state guidance are published tables and would test the crosswalk properly.
  6. Licence negotiations for HOWAS21 and the Multi-Coloured Manual, which are the two sources that would most improve the non-US prior.
  7. Full Bayesian inference on the hierarchy once the structure stops changing.

python tests/test_core.py && node tests/test_js_parity.js <site.html>

Every prediction carries a model version and an evidence snapshot hash over the source files, so a result can be tied to the exact inputs that produced it. The JavaScript engine shipped in the website is checked against the Python engine on 48 cases at each release; the current worst divergence is 1.2×10⁻⁷.
